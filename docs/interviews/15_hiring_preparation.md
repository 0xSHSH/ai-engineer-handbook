## Portfolio Checklist
- [ ] 2-4 projects, not 15 — depth beats breadth; a reviewer spends seconds per project on first pass.
- [ ] Each project has a one-paragraph "what problem does this solve" at the top, before any setup instructions.
- [ ] At least one project is deployed/live (not just a repo) — a working link removes all friction for a reviewer.
- [ ] Each project states the stack and your specific contribution if it was a team project.
- [ ] Screenshots or a short demo GIF for anything with a UI — reviewers rarely clone and run a project themselves.
- [ ] No broken links, no "TODO" sections, no dead API keys causing visible errors.

## LinkedIn Checklist
- [ ] Headline states role + specialization, not just job title (e.g., "AI Engineer — RAG systems & LLM infra" beats "Software Engineer").
- [ ] About section: 3-4 sentences, first-person, states what you build and what you're looking for next.
- [ ] Experience bullets mirror resume bullets (impact-first, quantified where possible) — don't just paste a job description.
- [ ] Skills section lists the actual technologies from your recent projects, ordered by relevance, not alphabetically.
- [ ] "Open to work" set appropriately for visibility if actively searching.
- [ ] Recent activity/posts (even occasional) signal you're active in the field — a dormant profile reads as disengaged.

## GitHub Checklist
- [ ] Pinned repos are your 4-6 best/most relevant projects, not whatever was pushed most recently.
- [ ] Every pinned repo has a real README (see the [project README template](../08_templates/project_readme_template.md)) — a repo with no README is effectively invisible to a reviewer.
- [ ] Contribution graph shows recent activity (green squares in the last 1-3 months) if actively job searching — gaps are fine, total silence for 6+ months raises a question.
- [ ] Profile README (the special `username/username` repo) gives a quick professional summary if you want extra polish.
- [ ] Commit history on flagship repos shows incremental, readable commits — not a single "final version" commit that hides your actual process.

## Networking Guide
- **Warm > cold, always**: a mutual connection's introduction converts at a far higher rate than a cold message — audit your existing network (alumni, past colleagues, online community members) before cold-outreaching strangers.
- **Give before you ask**: engage genuinely with someone's work (a thoughtful comment, sharing their post, a specific question about something they built) before requesting a referral or a call.
- **Target quality over volume**: 10 specific, well-researched outreach messages beat 100 copy-pasted ones — response rate on generic mass messages is close to zero.
- **Follow up once**: a single polite follow-up after ~1 week is appropriate; more than that reads as pressure, not persistence.

## Cold Email/Message Templates

**Referral request (warm-ish, e.g., alumni/community contact):**
> Hi [Name] — I saw you work on [specific team/project] at [Company]. I've been [specific relevant thing you built/learned], and I'm applying for [role] there. Would you be open to a quick 15-minute chat about your experience on the team, or, if it feels right after we talk, a referral? No worries at all if you're swamped — happy to just read anything you'd recommend instead.

**Cold outreach to an engineer at a target company:**
> Hi [Name] — I've been following [specific team's work / a specific launch/post], and it's exactly the kind of problem I want to work on. I recently built [one-line project description, with a link] tackling a related challenge. I'd love to hear what it's actually like working on [team] if you have 15 minutes sometime — and if a role opens up that fits, I'd be glad to be considered.

**Follow-up after no response (send once, after ~1 week):**
> Hi [Name] — following up in case this got buried. No pressure at all if now isn't a good time — I'll leave it here either way. Thanks for considering it!

## Referral Strategy
1. Map your existing network (LinkedIn 2nd-degree connections, alumni groups, past coworkers) against target companies before writing a single cold message.
2. Prioritize people on the *actual team* you're targeting over anyone at the company — an engineer on the hiring team's referral carries more internal weight than a random employee's.
3. Make the referrer's job easy: send them a short, ready-to-forward blurb (role link, 2-line summary of your fit) rather than asking them to write it from scratch.
4. A referral improves your odds of a first look — it does not replace interview prep. Treat it as getting through the door faster, not a shortcut past the actual bar.

## Salary Negotiation Guide
- **Never give a number first if you can avoid it** — deflect with "I'd love to understand the full range for this role first" or give a wide, well-researched range if pressed.
- **Research before the offer arrives**: use public comp data (levels.fyi-style aggregators, company-specific Glassdoor/Blind data) so your counter is grounded in real numbers for that company/level, not a guess.
- **Negotiate the whole package**, not just base: sign-on bonus, equity, start date, remote/relocation support, and level (a higher level can matter more long-term than a slightly higher base at a lower level).
- **Get competing offers if possible** — genuine leverage from a real competing offer is the single strongest negotiation lever; even a slower competing process can be mentioned honestly to create timeline flexibility.
- **Always negotiate in writing** once verbal terms are agreed — get the final offer in writing before resigning from a current role or declining other offers.
- **Stay collaborative, not adversarial**: frame it as "help me get to yes" rather than an ultimatum — most recruiters have some flexibility and are not offended by a reasonable, well-justified ask.

## Offer Comparison Framework
| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Base salary | Guaranteed, most liquid |
| Bonus (target %, historical payout rate) | Ask what % actually gets paid out historically, not just the target |
| Equity (RSU/options, vesting schedule, current strike/valuation) | For startups, model a realistic (not best-case) outcome; check cliff and vesting cadence |
| Level & scope | Affects both current comp and future promotion velocity |
| Team & manager | Ask to speak with the actual manager/team before accepting if you haven't already |
| Growth trajectory | Is this a team that's growing/strategic, or being sunset? |
| Work-life factors | On-call load, remote policy, travel expectations |

Build a simple weighted comparison rather than comparing only base salary — a lower base with strong equity at a high-growth company can meaningfully outweigh a higher base elsewhere, and vice versa depending on your risk tolerance.
